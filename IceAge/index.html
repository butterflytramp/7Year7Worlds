Six things people
are actually doing.

Most climate coverage is a countdown. This isn't. Below are six real interventions — each with a place, a date, the people running it, the numbers so far, and what is still unproven.

Every figure has a button next to it. Press it and you get the source, the publication date, and a plain-language note on how much weight it can carry. Where something is an estimate, it says so. Where specialists disagree, it says that too.

The point is simple: the planet is already being redesigned, by people you have mostly never heard of, and the work is checkable.

Proof

We fixed the sky once.

In 1987 the world found a hole in the ozone layer and signed the Montreal Protocol.

Nearly 99% of the controlled substances have been phased out. The layer is projected back to 1980 levels around 2040 for most of the world, 2045 over the Arctic, 2066 over Antarctica. The Kigali Amendment later extended it to refrigerants — up to 0.5°C of warming avoided.

A planetary problem, closed. It is the only one we have finished.

The catch

It took forty years, and it had to be policed — banned CFC-11 emissions were caught and traced three decades after signing. Ozone was also a narrower problem than energy: a handful of chemicals and manufacturers.

Making ice again

Pumps draw seawater from under the ice and spread it on top, where winter air freezes it in layers. Repeated through a season this makes a thicker, brighter patch that lasts further into summer.

Two teams are out on the Arctic sea ice in winter, pumping seawater up from underneath so it freezes thicker.

Who: Real Ice and Arctic Reflections, under ARIA's Re-thickening Arctic Sea Ice programme, led from the University of Cambridge.
Result so far: treated patches came out 45–50 cm thicker than the ice beside them, and brighter. First of three seasons.

People are standing on the Arctic ice, rebuilding it by hand.

Real IceArctic Reflections ARIAUniversity of Cambridge
The catch

One season, small patches. Whether it can scale is genuinely unknown, and several glaciologists think it can't. It also happens on Inuit sea ice, so who decides matters.

A reflective coating sends sunlight back instead of absorbing it. Painting every roof here moves the illustrative indoor temperature from 41°C toward 36°C — the scale reported in Ahmedabad, though real results vary by building and season.

A 2010 heatwave killed 1,344 people in Ahmedabad. The city's answer was mostly paint.

Who: Ahmedabad Municipal Corporation with IIPH Gandhinagar and NRDC. South Asia's first heat action plan, launched 2013 — warnings, hospital readiness, water access, cool roofs.
Result: reflective coatings on government buildings and thousands of informal-settlement roofs, dropping indoor temperatures by up to 5°C. Credited with preventing around 2,300 premature deaths; now running in over 100 Indian cities.

The cheapest climate technology may be a colour.

Ahmedabad Municipal CorporationIIPH Gandhinagar NRDC India100+ cities
The catch

The mortality figure is modelled, not counted. Coatings degrade, they do nothing for people who work outdoors, and reflecting heat does not cut emissions.

The only interaction here where you add nothing. Removing fences, hard surfacing and disturbance is often the whole job. Symbolic — real recovery depends on seed sources, hydrology and time, and does not always arrive.

In a lot of damaged places, the fix is to stop doing something.

Remove the fence, the concrete, the grazing pressure, the drainage — and the system often rebuilds itself faster than anyone expects. Mangrove replanting, Africa's Great Green Wall, reef restoration and Miyawaki plantings in Indian cities all work this way.

Sometimes the design is a decision to stop.

Great Green WallMangrove restoration Reef programmesMiyawaki forests
The catch

The evidence here is the weakest of the six. Mangroves work when hydrology and species choice are right and fail when they aren't. The Great Green Wall fell well short of its targets. And planting is routinely sold as an offset for emissions that should just be cut.

Montreal → Kigali1987 – present

Signing agreements

Eight parts, all required
Select each fragment to bring it into alignment · 0 of 8
Read as text

Eight parts — finding, ratification, phase-out schedule, funding, monitoring, trade measures, amendment, enforcement — must all align before anything happens. Two resist on the first attempt.

The least exciting item on this list, and the only one with a finished result.

Montreal worked because it was more than a promise: a binding phase-out schedule, a fund for developing countries, trade measures, monitoring, and a mechanism to make it stricter over time. Same machinery now sits behind the High Seas Treaty and the 30×30 biodiversity target — and the collapse in solar and battery costs has turned the energy transition into an economic argument rather than a moral one.

The most powerful climate technology ever deployed is a signed agreement.

The catch

Commitments are not outcomes. 30×30 and the High Seas Treaty are still at the ratification stage, and Montreal only held because someone kept checking.

Old cooling, reused

Passive cooling, in section
Shaded volume 42% · airflow moderate
Read as text

Deep reveals and a perforated jaali keep direct sun off the interior while letting light and air through. The courtyard and stepped water pull cooler air through by convection.

Before air conditioning, hot places were designed to be cool. Those designs still work.

Stepwells, jaali screens, courtyards, wind towers, thick walls. Not heritage decoration — thermal engineering, built by people with every incentive to get it right and no electricity to fall back on. The same logic covers sacred groves, and Indigenous fire management now being reintroduced in Australia and North America after a century of suppression.

A lot of climate design is retrieval, not invention.

The catch

These were built for particular climates and densities. They don't transfer unmodified to a high-rise city, or hold against wet-bulb extremes. And using the forms without crediting the communities who hold the knowledge is just extraction.

Honesty

What we're
not sure about.

If we only showed you the wins, you'd have no reason to believe the wins.

PreliminarySection 01

The Arctic numbers are one season old

45–50 cm came from the first of three winters, in a project update rather than peer-reviewed work. Arctic re-thickening works. Corrected to: it produced thicker, brighter ice on small patches, once.

ContestedSection 01

It may not scale, and it isn't our ice

Several glaciologists argue the approach can't reach a useful size and risks pulling attention off emissions. Energy cost and under-ice ecology are unanswered. And it happens on Inuit sea ice, where consent isn't a formality.

FailureVerified

The biggest carbon-capture plant has missed badly

Climeworks' Mammoth in Iceland is the largest direct air capture plant in the world. It has captured a small fraction of what it was built for since 2024, and the company has since made layoffs. It's here because leaving it out would make everything above less believable.

MethodHow to read this

What the labels mean

Measured — observed and reported. Estimated — modelled, with uncertainty. Preliminary — early, not yet reviewed. Projected — a forward scenario. Contested — specialists disagree. Interpretive — our opinion, not a finding.

In short

None of this is a plan. It's a list of things already working.

Six interventions, on four continents, run by people who mostly aren't famous. Some of it will fail. One of them already has. All of it is checkable, and the sources are below.

The planet is being redesigned whether or not anyone is watching. This is what that looks like in 2026.
